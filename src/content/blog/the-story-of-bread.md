Here is the full story of BREAD, told not as a list of dates, but as a narrative about a group of friends who tried to carve out a space for "weird" culture in a city that was rapidly becoming normal.

For the durable, research-friendly version of this material, use the [BREAD Archive index](/archive/).

## The Setting: San Francisco, 2014

To understand BREAD, you have to understand San Francisco in the mid-2010s. The second Tech Boom was exploding. The Mission District was changing from a Latino cultural hub into a playground for app developers.

The soundtrack to this gentrification was "Tech-House"—a genre of electronic music that is bouncy, inoffensive, and safe. It was the music of "work hard, play hard" patio parties. It was everywhere.

## The Spark: "Let's Break Things"

Two friends, Cage A (Kanav) and Prawns, decided they hated that soundtrack. They wanted to hear the music they were finding deep in the corners of SoundCloud—music from London and Chicago that was jagged, aggressive, and fast. Music that sounded like a panic attack you could dance to.

They formed a collective called BREAD. The name was simple, but the mission was specific: bring under-the-radar club music to venues that usually only booked the safe stuff.

## Chapter 1: The Experiment (2015)

On Friday, February 13, 2015, they threw their pilot event at a club called F8 on Folsom Street. They booked Helix, a producer from the Night Slugs label known for making music that sounded like metallic grinding.

It worked. They realized there was a hunger in San Francisco for something darker. They launched officially in May 2015. They were not just throwing parties; they were building a bridge between the internet and real life. They brought artists like Rabit and Doctor Jeep to SF—people who were famous online but had never played the city.

## Chapter 2: The Golden Era (2016)

2016 was the year BREAD hit its stride. They established a residency at F8, taking over the back room—a dark, sweaty box with a massive sound system.

This was their cool peak. They had a distinct aesthetic: high-contrast black-and-white flyers and temporary tattoos of their logo that people would wear at the rave.

**The big win:** In March 2016, they booked Jlin. She is now considered one of the greatest electronic composers alive (a Pulitzer nominee), but back then, she was an underground hero from Gary, Indiana. BREAD paid her a $1,000 guarantee—a huge risk for a small party—to bring her to SF for her debut.

**The sound:** They championed a genre called club construction. Imagine club music stripped of its melody, leaving only heavy drums, metallic clanks, and silence. It was hostile, but it was exciting.

## Chapter 3: The Bunker (2017)

The vibe shifted in late 2016. The Ghost Ship fire in Oakland (a tragedy where 36 people died in a DIY venue) caused the city to crack down on warehouses. Simultaneously, the Trump election shifted the mood of the country.

BREAD responded by moving to Underground SF (UGSF), a gritty, bunker-like venue in the Lower Haight.

The music changed, too. They moved away from the cold, metallic sounds of 2016 and started playing warmer, diasporic rhythms—Reggaeton, Dembow, and Kuduro. They booked artists like Kelman Duran, who mixed political speeches into dancehall beats. They were trying to create a safe space for Queer and Trans People of Color (QTPOC) bodies in a city that felt increasingly hostile.

## Chapter 4: The Network

BREAD was not just Cage A and Prawns. It grew into a family of residents, each bringing a different flavor:

- **Adam Kraft:** The party starter who played chaotic, pop-infused sets (he would go on to found the massive queer party FAKE and GAY).
- **Gewzer:** A producer from Fresno who drove hours to be part of the scene, bridging the gap between the Bay Area and the Central Valley.
- **Botaz & TF1000:** The technical wizards who kept the standards high.

They also ran a radio show, BREAD FM, on Radio Valencia, keeping the community alive on the airwaves between parties.

## Chapter 5: The Pivot & The Legacy (2021)

By 2018, life got in the way. Members started moving away or focusing on careers. The weekly grind stopped.

But when the pandemic hit in 2020 and nightlife shut down, BREAD came back—not to party, but to help. In 2021, they hosted a series of telethon streams on Twitch. They used their platform to raise over $12,000 for mutual aid funds, bail funds, and COVID relief in India.

## The Ending

BREAD did not fail; it completed its mission.

It proved you could play difficult music in SF and fill the room.

It incubated talent like Adam Kraft and Exteeng, who are now pillars of the SF art scene.

It ended on a high note of generosity, sending money to those who needed it most.

Today, the members are scattered across the globe—Tokyo, New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, and San Francisco. But for a few years, they created a glitch in the San Francisco system where the weirdos could still breathe.
